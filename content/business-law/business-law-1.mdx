## Business Law

### Liens

#### UCC-1 Lien

- A UCC-1 lien (a.k.a. UCC-1 financing statement) is a legal filing that a lender makes to publicly declare its security interest in a borrower's assets.
  -A UCC-1 doesn't create the loan—it perfects the lender's claim (it tells the world that the lender has a claim on the borrower's assets). It's a way for lenders to protect themselves by establishing priority over other creditors in case of default.
- A UCC-1 lien is typically filed when a borrower takes out a loan that is secured by collateral. The lender files the UCC-1 with the appropriate government office (usually the Secretary of State) to establish its claim on the collateral.
- The UCC-1 lien includes information about the borrower, the lender, and the collateral. It also specifies the terms of the loan and the security interest.
- Once a UCC-1 lien is filed, it becomes a public record. This means that other lenders and creditors can see that the borrower has a security interest in certain assets. If the borrower defaults on the loan, the lender with the UCC-1 lien has priority over other creditors in claiming the collateral.
- A UCC-1 lien can be released or terminated when the loan is paid off or when the lender agrees to release the lien. This is typically done by filing a UCC-3 termination statement with the appropriate government office.
- Priority - The first to file usually has priority in compensation during default
- Borrowing Restrictions - Existing UCC-1 filings can block new lenders and may require subordination agreements
- Risk signal - Multiple liens can indicate a distressed borrower, and expired or messy filings can indicate legal or operational risk.
- Example: A company takes out a loan to purchase equipment and uses the equipment as collateral. The lender files a UCC-1 lien to secure its interest in the equipment. If the company defaults on the loan, the lender with the UCC-1 lien has priority over other creditors in claiming the equipment.
- Example: A lender gives a $500K business loan and files a UCC-1 on “all assets.” If the business defaults, the lender can claim inventory, equipment, receivables, etc.
- Example: A borrower has multiple UCC-1 liens from different lenders. This can signal financial distress and may make it difficult for the borrower to secure additional financing.
- Common terms in a UCC-1 lien:
  - Debtor: The borrower who owes money to the lender.
  - Secured Party: The lender who has a security interest in the borrower's assets.
  - Collateral: The assets that are pledged as security for the loan.
  - Filing Date: The date when the UCC-1 lien is filed with the government office.
  - Expiration Date: The date when the UCC-1 lien expires if not renewed (usually five years from the filing date).
  - Perfection: The process of making a security interest legally enforceable against third parties, often achieved by filing a UCC-1 lien.
  - LIen Priority: The order in which creditors are paid in the event of a borrower's default, determined by the filing date of their UCC-1 liens.
  - UCC Search: A search of the public records to check for existing UCC-1 liens on a borrower before extending credit.
- Termination (UCC-3): A filing that releases a UCC-1 lien once the loan is repaid, indicating that the lender no longer has a claim on the collateral.
