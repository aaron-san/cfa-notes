Employee Equity Options and Compensation (Part 1)

Valuation

Equity-Based Compensation

  • Options create a potentially value-decreasing overhang over common stock values
  • There is a possibility that there will be more shares outstanding in the future
  • Stocks (both restricted and unrestricted) and equity options granted to employees have a dilutive effect on equity

Three forms of equity Compensation

  1. Stock or Equity
  2. Restrictied stock or equity - cannot be claimed or traded for a period after being granted
  3. Equity options - allows employees to buy stock in a firm at a specified price over a period (often with restrictions too)

Motivations for Offering Equity Compensation

  • Aligning stockholder and management goals (alleviating agency costs - managers tend to accumulate too much cash borrow too little and make unoptimal investments without the right incentives)
  • Cash deficiency
  • Employee retention - a vesting period (required period of stay with the company to unlock the granted stock or options) incentivizes employees to stay longer
  • Accounting and tax treatment
    • firms that use equity-based compensation have been allowed to report lower costs than firms that use cash-based compensation and there are sometimes laws that benefit firms that use options to reward employees
    • The exercise value of the option is tax deductible (rather than the entire value of the option)

Employee Compensation

  • Stocks options that are granted reduce the value of equity since a portion of the existing equity in the firm has to be set aside to meet these eventual option exercises
  • Expected option grants reduce the portion of expected future cash flows that accrue to existing stockholders and thus the value per share today.

Stock Options

Note:

Option Overhang is the percent of options outstanding relative to total shares.

  • The Investor Responsibility Research Center (IRRC) estimated the option overhang for 1,500 companies at all stages of the operating lifecycle to be 17 percent in 2003. - Firms that use options typically issue them every year with the strike price set to the prevailiing stock price (at-the-money when issued) - These options are usually long-term (typically 10-year maturities) - Options usually have vesting restrictions which require the employee to work for a stated period before having the ability to exercise the options - Options that are deep out-of-the-money are nonvested - Options are usually not transferable and are thus illiquid - Options typically must be exercised if an employee leaves the firm, assuming they are vested - For M&A transactions, outstanding options at a target firm are forcibly exercised

Accounting for Equity-based compensation

Stock Grants

  • The value of the stock grant is treated as a compensation expense when the grant is made and the number of shares increases in the firm

Stock Options (FASB 123R)

  • The FASB began requiring firms to expense options starting in 2006
  • Conventionally, firms could expense options only when they were exercised. The difference between the stock price and exercise price was expensed. This led to no expensing at the time of grant
  • When options are granted they must be modeled using one of the following models
    • Binomial lattice model
    • Black-Scholes
    • Monte Carlo simulations
  • The model must incorporate six inputs:
    1. Current stock price
    2. Price volatility
    3. Expected life considing maturity and vesting period
    4. Dividends
    5. Exercise price
    6. Riskless rate
  • The model can be adjusted to reflect specific characteristics of the options
  • Different models can be used to model different options within the same company
  • The option has to be adjusted for expected forefeitures (can be based on historical patterns of forfeiture and exercise)
  • The value of the options can be spread over the vesting period, starting with the year of grant (using straight-line or accelerated methods)
  • Option expenses usually reflect option expenses from prior years as well
  • If the actual forfeiture rate is different from the original estimate (used to value the options at grant), the option value must be reestimated and the expense must be adjusted

Valuation Principles

  • When a firm gives away something of value, we must record a transaction
  • Additional outstanding options leads to more volatile stockholder earnings, and stock or restricted stock compensation leads to less earnings variability over time
  • Options issued by firms are paid for by existing shareholders since the options dilute their value per share
  • Options issued by and listed on options exchanges have no effect on the value per share of the firms on which they are issued
  • Possible treatment of diluted shares
    1. Use diluted shares outstanding assuming they will all be exercised
    2. Use partially diluted shares (e.g, using only in-the-money options)
  • Many firms repurchase stock and set aside to cover option exercise rather than issue new shares (but this still has an effect on value from its effect on expected cash flows)

Accounting Treatment

  • FAS 123R requires firms to expense the fair value of stock options over the vesting period

Approaches to Valuing Employee Stock Options

  1. Adjust the number of shares to reflect options outstanding

    • Divide estimated value of equity (e.g., DCF value) by fully diluted shares outstanding
    • Overestimates exercise schedule since many options are out-of-the-money or nonvested
    • Underestimates value per share since it assumes all options are exercised
    • Does not incorporate expected proceeds from exercise which is a cash inflow to the firm
    • Does not consider the time permium on the options

  2. Forecast the option exercises and their effect on future shares outstanding

  3. Treasury Stock Method - extension of method 1 that accounts for the cash received from option exercises

  4. Value options at fair value rather than exercise value and subtract this value from total equity value in common stock

Expected Dilution

  • DCF already incorporates dilution
  • Do nothing!
  • Negative cash flows in early years reduces PV of operating assets (that reduction captures the dilution effect)

Computing Value Per Share with Effect of Dilution

  1. Value outstanding options as options (options pricing model) not assuming exercise (as many analysts do) to ensure value of timing option is incorporated which leads to lover value per share
  2. Divide value of equity by current shares outstanding (dilution already captured)
