## Principles of Valuation

- Anything can be valued
- Even the best valuations come with a substantial margin for error
- Investments must be perceived through the eyes of the marginal investor (investor most likely to be trading who is assumed to be well diversified)
- The discount rate should reflect the diversifed nature of the marginal investor and should only incorporate the nondiversifiable or market risk of the investment
- Principle of Parsimony - use the simplest model acceptable
- 4 Drivers of Valuation
  1. Cash flows from current assets (in place assets)
  2. Cash flow from growth assets (expected future investments)
  3. Risk
  4. Timing of the cash flows
- Aproaches to Valuation:
  1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Valuation
  2. Relative Valuation
  3. Contingent Claim Valuation (e.g., options valuation)
- Cash flows should be discounted at the rate that reflect their riskiness (e.g., income from cash should be discounted at the risk-free rate)
- Intrinsic value is the value of asset that a omniscient analyst would give using all available information and a perfect valuation model
- Methods of Valuation
  1. Valuing a business as a going concern
  2. Valuing a business as a collection of assets (e.g., liquidition value)
  - A liquidition discount reflects urgency of sale and is based on number of potential buyers, uniqueness of assets, and market conditions
  3. Valuing a business using excess returns and adjusted present value (APV)
  - The value of a firm will be the same under all three models if we make consistent assumptions
- Measures of Value
  - Value of the business (firm/enterprise value, using FCFF and cost of capital)
  - Value of equity (using FCFE and cost of equity)

## Purpose of Valuation

- Portfolio management
- Acquisition analysis
- Corporate finance
- Legal and tax purposes

## Biases in Valuation

- Choice of companies to value
- Information collection
- What other analyststs are saying (e.g., Zacks, IBES, First Call, etc.)
- Model selection
- Input selection - pessimistic or optimistic inputs (e.g., competition will drive margins to industry averages faster than expected)
- Postvaluation adjustments - trying to justify a pre-conceived notion of value
  - Minimizing divergance from current market price - anchoring bias
  - Adjust valuation upward using qualitative factors like synergy or premiums for control
  - Adjust the valuation down using qualitative factors like illiquidity or minority discounts (common in private equity valuation for tax and divorce court matters)
- Output interpretation, presentation, and decision making
- Institutinal pressures - about five times more buy recomemendations than sell recommendations (compensation often linked to recommendations) (M&A deals typically analyzed by acquiring firm's investment bank)
- There is no way of knowing how much of a valuation is attributable to the analyst's view of the macroeconomy and the view of the specific firm
- Analysts should value firm-specific components and try to remain neutral on macroeconomic variables (and update valuations as firm and macro variables change)

## Handling Uncertainty

- Better valuation models
- Valuation ranges instead of point estimates
- Probabilistic statements (e.g., There is a 70 percent chance that the stock is worth between $30 and $40, rather than a categorical statement that the stock is worth $35)
- Tools: simulations, decision trees, scenario analysis, sensitivity analysis

## Cost of Complexity

- Information overload
- Black Box Model - analysts no longer understand the inner workings of their model and use statements such as "The model says..." (beware of proprietary, commercial models)
- Difficulty discerning big assumptions versus small assumptions

## Excess Return Valuation Framework

- Value of firm = Capital invested in firm today + PV of excess return cash flows from both existing and future projects
- If capital invested is equal to the book value of capital, then firms that are expected to earn positive excess returns will have market values greater than book values and vice versa

## Adjusted Present Value (APV) Framework

- Value of firm = Value of unlevered firm + PV of financing side effects (e.g., tax shields, costs of financial distress)
- Value of firm = Value of firm with 100% equity financing + PV of expected tax benefits from debt - PV of expected costs of financial distress
- Useful when the capital structure is changing or when debt levels are high and the cost of financial distress is significant
- In DCF, effects of debt financing are captured in the discount rate, but in APV, monetary effects of debt financing are captured separate from operating assets

## Risk-free rate

- Usually a 10- or 30-year government bonds rates are used to proxy the risk-free rates (assumes the government won't default)
- 10-year Treasury bond rate

## Equity risk premium

- the premium that investors demand on an annual basis for investing in stocks instead of risk-free investments
- should be a function of how much risk is perceived in stocks and how concerned they are about that risk
- Between 1928 and 2010, stocks generated 4.31% more on an annual basis than treasury bonds.
- An alternative is to back out a forward-looking premium (called an implied equity risk premium) from current stock price levels and expected future cash flows.

## Relative risk or beta

- Generally estimate beta based on historical movement relative to the market (the slope of a regression of returns on the stock against a market index)
- These betas are always backward-looking and noisy (since they are estimated with error)
- One solution is to replace the regression beta with a sector-average beta, if the firm operates in only one business or a weighted average if it operates in several businesses.
  The sector beta is more precise than an individual regression beta because averaging across many betas results in averaging out your mistakes.

## Kinds of Expenses

1. Operating Expenses
   - labor, material
   - Expected to generate benefits only in current period
2. Capital Expenses
   - land, buildings, equipment
   - Expected to generate benefits over multiple periods
3. Financial Expenses
   - interest, principal payments, other nonequity financing costs

## Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)

- FCFE is the cash flow available to equity shareholders after all expenses, reinvestment, and debt repayments
- FCFE can be used to value equity directly using a discounted cash flow (DCF) model

<Math>
  {
    "FCFE = Net Income - (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - $\\Delta$ Noncash WC + (New Debt - Debt Repayments)"
  }
</Math>

## Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF)

- FCFF is the cash flow available to all capital providers (both debt and equity holders) after all expenses and reinvestment
- FCFF can be used to value the entire firm using a DCF model

<Math>
  {
    "FCFF = Operating Income (1 - Tax Rate) - (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - $\\Delta$ Noncash Working Capital"
  }
</Math>

## Goodwill

- "Goodwill is a result of acquisitions. It is the most useless intangible asset. It is a plug variable. Don’t pay for a plug variable." (Damodaran)
- Goodwill is an intangible asset that arises when a company acquires another company for a price higher than the fair value of its net identifiable assets.
- Goodwill is recorded on the balance sheet as an asset and represents the premium paid for factors such as brand reputation, customer relationships, and intellectual property.
- Goodwill is not amortized but is tested annually for impairment. If the carrying value of goodwill exceeds its fair value, an impairment loss is recognized in the income statement.

## Research and Development (R&D) Costs

- R&D expenses are often treated as operating expenses, but they should often be treated as capital expenditures, capitalized and depreciated for valuation purposes.
- R&D costs are expenses incurred in the process of discovering new knowledge, products, or processes.
- According to accounting standards, R&D costs are typically expensed as incurred, meaning they are expensed as incurred
- However, certain development costs may be capitalized if they meet specific criteria, such as technical feasibility and the intention to complete and use or sell the asset.
- Capitalized R&D costs are recorded as intangible assets on the balance sheet and amortized over their useful life.
- For valuation purposes, R&D costs whose benefits can be reasonably estimated to extend beyond the current period may be capitalized, while those that do not meet these criteria should be expensed immediately.

## Operating Leases

- Operating leases are often treated as operating expenses, but should be treated as financial expenses, capitalized, and added to debt for valuation purposes.
- Under new accounting standards (e.g., IFRS 16 and ASC 842), lessees are required to recognize a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the balance sheet for most leases, including operating leases.
- The debt value of an operating lease can be estimated by calculating the present value of future lease payments using the lessee's incremental borrowing rate or the rate implicit in the lease, if known.

## Reinvestment

- Firms must reinvest to maintain their growth

## Employee Stock Options (ESOs)

- Stock options outstanding that are held by employees and managers are reported in annual reports but not in quarterly reports, so assumptions must be made
- Employee stock options (ESOs) are a form of compensation that gives employees the right to purchase company stock at a predetermined price (exercise price) after a specified vesting period.
- ESOs can dilute existing shareholders' ownership when exercised, as new shares are issued to employees

## Right Issues (options to buy)

- Issuance of options to buy a specified number of ordinary shares over a specified period
- Given to existing shareholders
- 1-for-2 rights issue - allows shareholders to purchase one new share for every two shares they currently own
- Can be dilutive to existing shareholders since it potentially increases the number of shares outstanding and thus reduces the value per share
- transferable/tradeable - can be sold in the (OTC) market, so they have value even if not exercised
- Subscription price is usually set at a discount to the current market price to encourage shareholders to exercise their rights and purchase the new shares

- TTM refers to the financial performance of a company over the most recent 12-month period, regardless of the fiscal year-end.

### Multiples and Drivers

1. PE ratio - E(g), payout, risk
2. P/BV_equity - E(g), payout, risk, ROE
3. P/Sales - E(g), payout, risk, net margin
4. EV/FCFF - cost of capital, growth rate
5. EV/EBITDA - E(g), reinvestment rate, risk, ROIC, tax rate
6. EV/capital - E(g), reinvestment rate, risk, ROIC
7. EV/Sales - E(g), reinvestment rate, risk, operating margin

1. PE ratio - E(g) - Low PE with high E(g) in EPS

3. P/Sales - Net margin - Low P/S with high net profit margin

- Multiples should be used with comparable firms (firms with similar cash flows, growth potential, and risk)
- Analysts often compare companies in sectors, industries, or businesses and assume they are similar in respect to cash flows, growth and risk
- Average or medians of multiples are compared to those of the sector or other benchmark
